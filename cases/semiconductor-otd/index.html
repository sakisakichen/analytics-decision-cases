CASE 01 · SEMICONDUCTOR / MANUFACTURING

Production Was Healthy. Why Was On-Time Delivery Falling?

A business-first diagnostic case tracing a six-week decline in customer delivery performance from KPI definition to an operational bottleneck—and then to a decision that preserves the business control without preserving the wait.

Operations Analytics Metric Definition Data Grain Process Analytics Root Cause Analysis
CUSTOMER OUTCOME 95% → 88% On-Time Delivery
PRODUCTION 93% → 92% Completed by required date
POST-PRODUCTION 1.1 → 2.6 days Production Complete → Ship
01 · BUSINESS PROBLEM

The factory looked healthy. The customer outcome did not.

A mid-sized semiconductor company's On-Time Delivery had deteriorated for six consecutive weeks. Production output showed no obvious decline, equipment utilization remained normal to high, yet customer escalations about late deliveries were increasing.

Business question:
Where in the order-to-delivery lifecycle is the delay occurring, and where should Operations intervene?
02 · DEFINE THE KPI

Before diagnosing OTD, define what “on time” means.

The company measures OTD at the Customer Order Line grain. The full committed quantity must be delivered on or before the promised delivery date. A partial delivery does not count as an on-time order line.

1,000 units committed 700 delivered by promise date
→
OTD Flag = Late On-time quantity = 70%, but order-line OTD = 0

Metric: OTD % = On-Time Eligible Order Lines ÷ Total Eligible Order Lines.

The report uses the latest promised delivery date. Promise-date revision behavior was checked as a governance edge case; no material change was found that could explain the recent decline.

03 · SOURCE & GRAIN

Three operational systems. Three different grains.

OMS Order Line Promise, product, committed quantity
MES Production Lot Completion events and quantities
WMS / TMS Shipment Line Shipment and delivery events

One order line may be fulfilled by multiple production lots and multiple shipment lines. A direct raw join can therefore create fanout and double-count quantities. The analysis first reconciles operational events back to the business grain needed for OTD.

Modeling principle: Do not build a fact table just because one can be built. Model only as much as needed to produce trustworthy analysis; operationalize further when reuse and governance justify it.
04 · FIND WHERE

Work backward from the customer outcome.

METRIC PREVIOUS 6 WEEKS RECENT 6 WEEKS
OTD 95% 88%
Production completed by required date 93% 92%
Production Complete → Ship 1.1 days 2.6 days
Ship → Delivery 2.0 days 2.1 days

Production completion and transportation were nearly stable. The deterioration was concentrated after production and before shipment.

OTD ↓ → Production stable → Production → Ship ↑ → Warehouse Processing ↑ → Product C
PRODUCT FAMILY PREVIOUS RECENT
Product A 0.2 day 0.3 day
Product B 0.2 day 0.3 day
Product C 0.3 day 3.1 days
Product D 0.2 day 0.4 day
05 · FIND WHY

A new control changed the fulfillment process.

About seven weeks earlier, Product C gained a Manual Allocation Review . The control was introduced to prevent constrained inventory from being consumed by standard orders before strategic-customer demand could be protected.

Before: Packing Complete → Warehouse Queue → Inventory Ready

After: Packing Complete → Manual Allocation Review → Warehouse Queue → Inventory Ready

STAGE BEFORE AFTER
Packing → Allocation Review Start N/A 1.8 days
Allocation Review Processing N/A 0.2 day
Review Complete → Warehouse Queue N/A 0.7 day
Warehouse Queue → Ready 0.3 day 0.4 day
Total 0.3 day 3.1 days
“The bottleneck wasn't the work. It was the wait.” PROCESSING TIME ≠ WAITING / QUEUE TIME

The review itself required only about 0.2 day. Most of the delay came from waiting to enter the review and waiting again after review. Production volume, quality, transportation, and promise-date revision behavior were also checked and did not show changes large enough to explain the deterioration.

06 · BUSINESS DECISION

Preserve the control. Remove unnecessary waiting.

Removing Manual Allocation Review immediately would ignore why it exists: protecting scarce inventory for strategic customers. The first decision question is therefore: How often does the review actually change an allocation decision?

STANDARD / RULE-BASED Auto Allocation → Warehouse

Route normal orders directly when allocation rules can make the decision reliably.

CONFLICT / AMBIGUITY Manual Review → Warehouse

Escalate only true exceptions that require human judgment.

This converts a 100% manual-review workflow into an exception-based control. If the review rarely changes allocation and existing system rules already protect priority customers, full removal can then be evaluated.

07 · VALIDATE THE DECISION

A successful change must improve speed without recreating the original risk.

Pilot the automated / exception-based flow for a subset of Product C orders and evaluate both service performance and control effectiveness.

Warehouse Processing Time ↓ Move back toward the historical ~0.3-day level.
OTD ↑ Recover toward the historical 94–96% range.
Allocation Errors ↔ Do not increase strategic-customer inventory failures.

CASE TAKEAWAY

Start from the customer outcome. Separate WHERE from WHY. Understand the control before removing the bottleneck.

The case began with a factory that looked healthy and a customer KPI that did not. The useful answer came from defining the metric, reconciling grain, tracing the process, separating processing time from waiting time, and connecting the root cause to a decision that respected the business constraint.
