CASE 01 / OPERATIONS ANALYTICS / SEMICONDUCTOR

Production was healthy.
Why was on-time delivery falling?

A six-week decline in customer delivery performance looked like a manufacturing problem. Working backward from the customer outcome revealed something different: the bottleneck was created after production by a newly introduced fulfillment control.

CUSTOMER SIGNAL 95% → 88% On-Time Delivery
BREAKPOINT 1.1 → 2.6 days Production Complete → Ship
ROOT CAUSE Queue / Wait Manual Allocation Review workflow

THE INVESTIGATION

Start with the customer outcome. Work backward through the process.

The objective was not to build a dashboard first. It was to define the KPI correctly, reconcile operational data at the right grain, locate where performance broke down, identify why, and connect the evidence to an operational decision.

01
BUSINESS PROBLEM

The factory looked healthy. The customer outcome did not.

A mid-sized semiconductor company's On-Time Delivery had deteriorated for six consecutive weeks. Production output showed no obvious decline, equipment utilization remained normal to high, yet customer escalations about late deliveries were increasing.

BUSINESS QUESTION Where in the order-to-delivery lifecycle is the delay occurring, and where should Operations intervene?
02
METRIC DEFINITION

Before diagnosing OTD, define what “on time” means.

OTD is measured at the Customer Order Line grain. The full committed quantity must be delivered on or before the promised delivery date. A partial delivery does not count as an on-time order line.

1,000 units committed 700 arrive by promised date
→
OTD Flag = Late Quantity on time = 70%, but order-line OTD = 0
OTD % = On-Time Eligible Order Lines ÷ Total Eligible Order Lines

The operational report uses the latest promised delivery date. Promise-date revision behavior was therefore treated as a metric-governance edge case and checked before attributing the decline to operations.

03
SOURCE & GRAIN

Three systems. Three different operational grains.

OMS Order Line Customer promise, product and committed quantity
MES Production Lot Manufacturing completion events and quantities
WMS / TMS Shipment Line Shipment and delivery events

One order line can be fulfilled by multiple production lots and multiple shipment lines. Joining the raw systems directly can therefore create fanout and double-count quantities. Operational events first need to be reconciled back to the business grain required by the KPI.

Modeling principle: build only as much analytical structure as needed to produce trustworthy analysis. A reusable fact or semantic model becomes valuable when the use case needs ongoing governance and reuse; it is not automatically required for every diagnostic case.
04
FIND WHERE

Work backward from the customer outcome.

Production completion remained nearly unchanged, and transportation after shipment was also stable. The largest deterioration occurred after production and before shipment.

05
FIND WHY

Product C had gained a new fulfillment control.

About seven weeks earlier, the company introduced a Manual Allocation Review for Product C. The control was designed to prevent constrained inventory from being consumed by standard orders before strategic-customer demand could be protected.

The bottleneck wasn't the work. It was the wait. PROCESSING TIME ≠ WAITING / QUEUE TIME

The manual review itself took only about 0.2 day. The larger delay came from approximately 1.8 days waiting to enter the review and another 0.7 day waiting after the review before entering the warehouse queue. Production volume, production quality, transportation, and promise-date revision behavior did not show changes large enough to explain the deterioration.

06
BUSINESS DECISION

Preserve the control. Remove unnecessary waiting.

Simply removing Manual Allocation Review would ignore why the control exists: protecting scarce inventory for strategic customers. The better decision is to first determine how often human review actually changes an allocation.

How can we preserve priority-allocation control without forcing every Product C order through the same manual queue?
STANDARD CASE Auto Allocation → Warehouse

Allow rule-based orders to move directly when the allocation decision is clear.

EXCEPTION Manual Review → Warehouse

Send only inventory conflicts or ambiguous allocation decisions to human review.

This changes the operating model from 100% of orders → Manual Review to an exception-based workflow.

07
VALIDATE THE DECISION

Faster is only better if the original business risk remains controlled.

Pilot the automated / exception-based allocation workflow on a subset of Product C orders and evaluate both service performance and control effectiveness.

Processing Time ↓ Move warehouse processing back toward the historical ~0.3-day level.
OTD ↑ Recover toward the historical 94–96% range.
Allocation Risk ↔ Do not increase strategic-customer inventory allocation failures.
CASE 01 / TAKEAWAY

Start from the outcome.
Separate WHERE from WHY.
Understand the control before removing it.

The case started with a factory that looked healthy and a customer KPI that did not. The useful answer came from defining the metric, reconciling grain, tracing the process, separating processing time from waiting time, and connecting the root cause to a decision that respected the original business constraint.
